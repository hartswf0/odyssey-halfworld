The Film Is the Program

Procedural epic, the algorithmic camera, and the drift toward description

Prepared for submission · Histories of Media Art, Science and Technology

An epic — Homer's Odyssey, all twenty-four books — was reconstructed not as recorded images but as executable code. One hundred and fifty-two scenes and four hundred and thirty visual assets exist as procedural modules: short programs that regenerate their pictures on demand, in ordered-dot halftone, from rules a reader can inspect. The result is a 164-minute film whose entire archive is source text. This paper reports what the attempt taught, including three failures that took months to see.

1The proposition

In 1963 at Bell Laboratories, Kenneth Knowlton wrote BEFLIX, a language for making films. The moving image was not captured; it was notated — written as instructions that a machine executed into frames of dots. The proposition embedded there has been largely abandoned: that a film can be a readable program, and therefore a teachable one.

This project takes that proposition literally and asks what happens when it meets contemporary generative systems — when the notation is written not by one author at a console but by orchestrated language models, and when the corpus grows faster than any individual can inspect it.

Every image in the work is code. A scene is roughly two hundred lines declaring who is present, where they stand, and what happens over time; it draws itself when asked. Nothing is a photograph of a render. The archive is the source, and the film is what the source does.

2Method

The system is organised around five interchange contracts governing who exists (CAST), where things stand (STAGE), when events occur (SCORE), who decides (MIND), and what persists (MEMORY). Each is plain JSON. A governing rule holds that a machine actor receives exactly the powers a hand receives: the same verbs, the same validation, the same clamps.

Assets and scenes were produced by fleets of agents running a mandatory loop — write the module, render it, read the rendered image, revise — with two standing constraints. All generation is additive: earlier work is never modified, only extended. And all generation is deterministic: the same source yields the same film, so the work can be re-recorded frame for frame rather than merely replayed.

Speech, blocking and camera are derived rather than authored shot by shot. Because the system knows who speaks, exactly when (from measured audio), and where that body stands (from a room authored once in top-down plan space), a camera becomes a computable crop rather than a hand animation.

3Three findings

3.1 Generation at scale drifts toward description

The characters, driven directly by machine-generated performance data, did not speak. They narrated stage directions about themselves. Seven hundred and six dramatic turns carried instructions of the form "Athena redirects the council toward Odysseus" in the place where an utterance belonged — and, more consequentially, the synthesised voice track spoke those instructions aloud, in character, with feeling.

The defect was invisible to every automated check: the data was well-formed, complete, and semantically accurate. It was wrong only in the sense that matters. Repair required authoring a separate corpus of first-person speech for all 706 turns, then re-recording every voice.

Language models asked to produce a performance reliably produce an account of a performance. The difference is inaudible to validation and obvious to an ear.

3.2 Abundance produces inventory, not composition

The title sequence was built three times. The first assembled all 886 available renders into a photographic mosaic of the work's name: complete, verifiable, inert. At roughly eight pixels per tile, no image could be seen — the archive was hidden by its own abundance. The second abandoned the corpus entirely for a reductive graphic line, and threw away the material.

Only the third succeeded, by treating the corpus as material rather than as evidence. It is a canonical BEFLIX frame — 128 × 96 cells at eight ink levels — in which every dot is a live scene, cycling its own moments. The cells then migrate: a true permutation, nothing substituted, every instance present at the start still present at the end, until the tonal pattern precipitates into the title. Legibility measured 99.2% against the letterform mask, with contrast a third higher than the exhaustive version.

Completeness and composition were found to be actively opposed. The corpus became legible only when it was permitted to reorganise rather than merely to accumulate.

3.3 Constraint produces access

The commitment that all geometry bottoms out in ink dots yields artefacts of unusual lightness. The exhaustive title required 886 network requests and 36 megabytes; on an emulated 4 Mbit/s link at 100 ms latency it never finished loading — 505 of 893 files after fifteen minutes. Recomposed as sprite atlases, the same sequence loads in fifteen seconds at 3.3 megabytes, and the final BEFLIX title is 590 kilobytes across three requests.

Where synthetic visual culture increasingly assumes abundant bandwidth, a dot-matrix substrate is not nostalgia but infrastructure. The claim is advanced cautiously, and with figures.

4Two failures of machine perception

Both persisted for months. Both were invisible to validation and visible only to sustained looking.

The effects were frozen. Fifty-six of sixty effect modules expect a normalised progress value across their active window; every caller handed them the scene clock in seconds. Each internal clamp therefore pinned at its maximum after the first second. For the entire history of the project, every flare, corridor and sweep displayed only its final frame. They were never badly drawn; they had simply never been advanced.

The camera followed the wrong body. The performance corpus attributes roughly three quarters of its segments to a narrator, because the narrator performs the action beats. A camera keyed to the nominal speaker would therefore have sat in wide shot for most of the film — technically correct, cinematically dead. The true subject had to be recovered from a different layer of the same data.

6The artefacts

The paper's claims are checkable because the work is running. Each of the following is a single self-contained page.

  • The dot law, literally →
    A 128×96 BEFLIX frame in which every dot is a live scene. The corpus opens in the film's own order, then migrates until the ink pattern becomes the work's name; descending into a single letter resolves the dots back into full renders.
  • The line →
    The reductive counter-proposal: one rule as the voyage, a harmonic family for the wandering, an inversion for the underworld, and a return measured to be pixel-identical to the departure.
  • All cuts, one clock →
    Every rendering of the poem playing simultaneously against a single master clock — the scene's own composition, the unified restage, an algorithmic camera that follows the speaker, and the performance view — so the grammars can be compared rather than argued about.
  • The long listen →
    The work as audio: 152 studio-voiced scenes on one clock, scored, built for a car on a road with no signal.
  • Walk the book →
    The same corpus as a walkable world, in which the props, set pieces and sound sources are affordances rather than scenery.
  • Methods, figure by figure →
    The dot law, keying, restage, camera and reassembly, each as a live figure rather than a diagram of one.
  • The studio →
    The five contracts as a harmonic family; the title that becomes its own index.

7Limits, and what is not claimed

No decolonial method is claimed. The material is canonical European; the tooling depends on commercial inference infrastructure concentrated in the Global North; the voices are synthesised by a proprietary model. These are dependencies the project names rather than resolves, and they bound what the third finding can mean.

Nor is the corpus finished in any strong sense. Ninety-nine of one hundred and fifty-two stages are reconstructions rather than authored blocking, and are flagged as such in the data so that a reconstruction is never mistaken for a decision. When ported to the studio's contracts, the rooms did not survive: the receiving specification has no field for a backdrop, so the port yields the right bodies standing in the right places in an empty ground. It is, for now, a cast-and-blocking corpus rather than a world — which is itself a finding about what such contracts must grow.

What the work offers is narrower than a manifesto and, it hopes, more useful: a demonstration that a computationally generated moving image can be archived as its own source, distributed under severe bandwidth constraint, and read — and modified — by anyone able to read a page of code.
